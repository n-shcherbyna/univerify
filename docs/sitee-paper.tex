\begin{abstract}
Blockchain can make academic credentials independently
verifiable, yet on Ethereum the cost of persistent storage
threatens practicality.
This paper quantifies that cost in \emph{UniVerify}, a
prototype diploma-verification system, by comparing two
on-chain representations:
\emph{packed per-record storage}, where each diploma
occupies one slot, and
\emph{Merkle batch issuance}, where an entire graduation
cohort is represented by a single root.

Four contract variants are benchmarked---\Vpacked{},
\Vbase{}, \Voz{}, and \Vopt{}---across proof depths
$d \in \{0,1,4,8,10,12\}$.
Merkle batching reduces per-diploma issuance cost by
98.9\% at batch size $N{=}100$, shifting part of the cost
to proof-based verification.
\Vopt{} lowers verification gas by 6.6\% versus \Vbase{}
and by 3.7\% versus \Voz{}.
A closed-form gas-savings model is derived and validated at
six proof depths with residuals below 0.2\%.
Under mainnet conditions
(40~gwei, 1\,ETH\,=\,\$2{,}500), issuing 1{,}000 diplomas
costs \$4{,}820 per-record versus \$5.09 with batching.
The results show that architecture dominates
micro-optimization for institutional diploma issuance.
\end{abstract}

\begin{IEEEkeywords}
Ethereum, Solidity, gas optimization, Merkle tree,
smart contracts, diploma verification
\end{IEEEkeywords}

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

The global market for fraudulent academic credentials is
estimated to exceed one billion dollars
annually~\cite{ezell2012degree}, yet cross-border diploma
verification still relies on slow, manual contact with
the issuing university.
Blockchain-based registries offer an alternative:
once a credential commitment is published on-chain, any
party can validate it independently, without relying on a
single institutional database.

The main challenge is economic.
On Ethereum, each persistent storage write costs roughly
20{,}000~gas.
If every diploma is stored individually, institutions
issuing hundreds or thousands of diplomas per cycle face
prohibitive costs.
The key question is therefore not \emph{whether} blockchain
can be used, but \emph{how credentials should be
represented on-chain so that issuance remains practical}.

This paper studies that question through
\emph{UniVerify}, a prototype diploma-verification system.
Two architectures are compared:
\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{Per-record storage} --- each diploma is
        stored in its own mapping slot, verified in
        constant time.
  \item \textbf{Merkle batch issuance} --- one Merkle
        root represents an entire cohort; each diploma is
        verified via an off-chain proof.
\end{enumerate}

The contribution is practical rather than conceptual:
this work quantifies the gas implications of deployable
Solidity designs and derives a closed-form model predicting
optimization savings across proof depths.

\subsection{UniVerify System}

UniVerify is a prototype comprising a Solidity smart
contract for on-chain storage and verification, and an
off-chain TypeScript SDK for Merkle tree construction and
proof generation.
Universities act as \emph{issuers} who publish diploma
commitments on-chain; a verifier checks a diploma using
the document hash and, in the Merkle variant, a proof of
inclusion in the published batch root.

\subsection{Contributions}

\begin{enumerate}
  \item A quantitative comparison of packed per-record
        storage and Merkle-batch issuance across deployment,
        issuance, verification, and revocation cost.
  \item A four-way benchmark of \Vpacked{}, \Vbase{},
        \Voz{}~\cite{openzeppelin}, and \Vopt{}, separating
        architectural, library, and EVM-level effects.
  \item A closed-form gas-savings model validated at six
        proof depths with residuals below 0.2\%.
  \item An economic analysis translating gas savings into
        institutional issuance cost.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Related Work}
\label{sec:related}

Grech and Camilleri~\cite{grech2017blockchain} identified
credential verification as a primary educational
blockchain application.
Lizcano et~al.~\cite{lizcano2020decentralised} proposed a
decentralized credential framework on IPFS and Ethereum
but omitted low-level cost analysis.
Alketbi et~al.~\cite{alketbi2018blockchain} surveyed
broader blockchain use in public-sector services,
including education records.
More recently, the W3C Verifiable Credentials
standard~\cite{w3cvc2024} defines a data model for
tamper-evident claims but is agnostic to cost;
Arndt et~al.~\cite{arndt2023onchain} benchmarked
on-chain credential anchoring on Ethereum~L2 chains
without comparing Merkle batching to per-record storage.

Merkle trees~\cite{merkle1987} provide the standard
mechanism for compact set-membership proofs.
OpenZeppelin's \texttt{MerkleProof}~\cite{openzeppelin}
is the de~facto production Solidity library.
Alternative structures---sparse Merkle trees, Verkle
trees~\cite{verkle}, RSA accumulators---remain less
practical for ordinary EVM contracts.

Ethereum execution costs follow the EVM gas
schedule~\cite{yellowpaper}; EIP-2929 further raised
cold state-access costs~\cite{eip2929}.
Most prior work on blockchain credentials focuses on
architecture, privacy, or governance.
By contrast, this paper isolates a narrower question:
how specific Solidity design choices affect issuance and
verification cost in a deployable credential registry.

\section{System Design and Methodology}
\label{sec:method}

\subsection{Two Verification Architectures}

Fig.~\ref{fig:arch} illustrates the two approaches.

\smallskip\noindent\textbf{Architecture~A --- Per-Record
Storage.}
Each diploma is stored in a
\texttt{mapping(bytes32\,$\Rightarrow$\,uint256)}.
The issuer address (160~bits) and a revocation flag (1~bit)
are packed into one 256-bit slot.
Issuance requires one \texttt{SSTORE} per diploma;
verification is a single \texttt{SLOAD} plus bit masking,
yielding constant read cost.
This contract is intentionally minimal and serves as a gas
baseline.

\smallskip\noindent\textbf{Architecture~B --- Merkle Batch.}
All diplomas in a cohort form a Merkle tree off-chain.
Only the 32-byte batch root is stored on-chain via
\texttt{issueBatchRoot}, so one state write covers the
entire batch.
Verification requires the caller to supply
$d{=}\lceil \log_2 N \rceil$ sibling hashes.
Each leaf is domain-separated:
$\texttt{keccak256}(\mathit{contract}\|\mathit{chainId}
\|\mathit{issuer}\|\mathit{batchId}\|\mathit{docHash})$,
preventing cross-contract and cross-chain replay.

\subsection{Optimization Techniques}
\label{sec:techniques}

The \Vopt{} variant targets two internal functions:
\texttt{\_processProof()} and \texttt{\_merkleLeaf()}.

\smallskip\noindent\textbf{T1 --- Unchecked loop.}
\texttt{unchecked\{++i;\}} removes overflow-check overhead.

\smallskip\noindent\textbf{T2 --- Scratch-space hashing.}
The two 32-byte inputs are written to EVM scratch space
and hashed in place, avoiding \texttt{abi.encodePacked}
memory allocation.

\smallskip\noindent\textbf{T3 --- Validation splitting.}
The public \texttt{merkleLeaf()} checks inputs; a private
\texttt{\_merkleLeaf()} is used internally.

\smallskip\noindent\textbf{T4 --- Assembly preimage.}
The 112-byte leaf preimage is packed via
\texttt{mstore}/\texttt{shl} instead of
\texttt{abi.encodePacked}.

\subsection{Experimental Setup}

All measurements use Foundry~\cite{foundry2023}
(Solidity~0.8.20, optimizer at 200~runs).
Merkle trees are built on-chain only in the test harness;
in deployment they are constructed off-chain.
Proof depths $d \in \{0,1,4,8,10,12\}$ correspond to batch
sizes $2^0$ to $2^{12}$ (1--4{,}096 diplomas).

Publishing one Merkle batch root costs 50{,}939~gas

$50{,}939/N$.

\subsection{Economic Interpretation}

With gas price $g{=}40$~gwei and $p{=}\$2{,}500$/ETH:
\begin{equation}
\mathrm{USD} = \mathrm{gas} \cdot g \cdot p \cdot 10^{-9}
\label{eq:usd}
\end{equation}

This yields \$4.82 per diploma for \Vpacked{} issuance
and \$5.09 for one Merkle batch root.
At cohort scale the difference is dramatic:
100 diplomas cost \$482 per-record versus \$5.09 batched;
1{,}000 diplomas cost \$4{,}820 versus \$5.09.
These figures are sensitive to network conditions:
at 10~gwei / \$1{,}500 (bear market) the 1{,}000-diploma
per-record cost drops to \$723, while at 80~gwei /
\$4{,}000 (peak congestion) it rises to \$15{,}424.
The Merkle batch cost stays below \$17 in all scenarios,
confirming that the architectural advantage holds
across a wide range of market conditions.

\subsection{Closed-Form Savings Model}

The gas savings between \Vbase{} and \Vopt{} decompose
into contributions from the four techniques.
Let $d$ denote proof depth:
\begin{equation}
\Delta(d) =
  \underbrace{2^d \cdot 42}_{\text{T4}}
  +\underbrace{96}_{\text{T3}}
  +\underbrace{d \cdot 199}_{\text{T1+T2}}
  +\underbrace{42}_{\text{base}}
\label{eq:model}
\end{equation}

The constant 42 (``base'') accounts for the fixed-cost
savings in leaf construction that are independent of
proof depth.
Table~\ref{tab:model} validates the model against
measured gas differences via \texttt{revokeFromBatch},
which exercises the same proof-verification hot path as
\texttt{statusWithProof}.

\section{Discussion and Conclusion}
\label{sec:discussion}

\subsection{Engineering Trade-off}

The dominant design decision is architectural.
\Vpacked{} minimizes verification cost but scales linearly
with the number of issued diplomas.
Merkle batching inverts this relationship: issuance becomes
nearly constant at batch level, at the expense of
proof-based verification.
For diploma registries this trade-off is favorable---issuance
is a paid institutional operation, while verification is a
read-only action repeated over the credential lifetime.

\subsection{Optimization Hierarchy}

Three layers of optimization emerge:
\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{Architecture}:
        switching to Merkle batching reduces issuance cost
        by up to 98.9\% per diploma.
  \item \textbf{Code-level tuning}:
        \Vopt{} reduces verification gas by 6.6\% and
        deployment gas by 10.8\% vs.\ \Vbase{}.
  \item \textbf{Library choice}:
        \Voz{} reduces verification gas by 3.1\%
        vs.\ \Vbase{}.
\end{enumerate}

Micro-optimizations yield measurable gains but cannot
compensate for an inefficient storage model.
The largest improvement comes from eliminating per-record
state writes.

\subsection{Practical Implications}

For small-scale or infrequent issuance, per-record storage
remains attractive due to its simple logic and
constant-time lookup.
For cohort-scale institutional use, Merkle batching is
the practical choice.

OpenZeppelin's \texttt{MerkleProof} is a sensible
production default---it captures most available gains while
preserving readability and auditability.
The additional savings of \Vopt{} justify the complexity
only when proof verification is a critical path.

\subsection{Limitations and Future Work}

This study is limited to gas efficiency on Ethereum~L1;
it does not address privacy, governance, or
interoperability.
All gas measurements use Foundry's local EVM, where
storage slots are warm after first access within a
transaction; on mainnet, EIP-2929 cold-access surcharges
(2{,}100~gas per first \texttt{SLOAD}) would increase
absolute verification cost but preserve the relative
rankings among variants.
The packed per-record contract is a simplified baseline,
so the comparison should be read as a cost study rather
than a feature-identical system evaluation.

Future work includes benchmarking on Layer~2 networks,
studying calldata-sensitive designs under EIP-4844, and
evaluating Verkle-based verification once production EVM
support is available.

\subsection{Conclusion}

This paper compared two deployable architectures for
on-chain diploma verification and derived a closed-form
model predicting optimization gains across proof depths.
The central finding is that \emph{architecture dominates
micro-optimization}: institutions issuing more than a
handful of diplomas per cycle should adopt Merkle batching,
which reduces per-diploma cost by two orders of magnitude.
For the verification path, OpenZeppelin's
\texttt{MerkleProof} offers the best trade-off between gas
savings and code auditability; hand-tuned assembly
(\Vopt{}) is justified only under extreme throughput
requirements.
As credential anchoring moves to Layer~2 networks and
EIP-4844 reshapes calldata economics, the batching
advantage will only grow---making Merkle-based registries
the robust default for blockchain-backed academic
credentials.
