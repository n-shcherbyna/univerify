\begin{abstract}
Fake academic diplomas are a growing problem worldwide,
yet checking whether a diploma is real still requires
slow, manual contact with the issuing university.
Blockchain registries can solve this by letting anyone
verify a credential on-chain---but on Ethereum, storing
data is expensive, so the way diplomas are represented
in a smart contract matters greatly.

This paper examines these costs using \emph{UniVerify},
a diploma-verification system built on Ethereum.
Two designs are compared: \emph{per-record storage},
where each diploma takes one contract slot, and
\emph{Merkle-batch issuance}, where a whole graduation
class is covered by a single cryptographic root.
To separate the effect of the architecture from
code-level choices, four Solidity contract variants
are tested with batch sizes from 1 to 4{,}096 diplomas.

The difference is substantial.
Merkle batching cuts the per-diploma issuance cost by
98.9\,\% for a class of 100 students and by three orders
of magnitude at 1{,}000 students---from \$4{,}820 down
to \$5.09 at current Ethereum prices.
Low-level EVM optimizations save another 6.6\,\% on
verification gas, but they are far less important than
the choice of architecture.
A closed-form model is also derived that decomposes the
optimization savings into a per-step and a constant term
and matches measured gas values with errors below 0.2\,\%.

The main conclusion is that for universities that issue
diplomas in batches, the data structure used on-chain
is the most important cost factor---not low-level code
tuning---and Merkle-batch registries are the practical
default for blockchain-based academic credentials.
\end{abstract}

\begin{IEEEkeywords}
Ethereum, Solidity, gas optimization, Merkle tree,

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

The market for fake academic credentials is estimated
at over one billion dollars per year~\cite{ezell2012degree}, 
but verifying whether a diploma is genuine still depends on 
contacting the issuing university---a process that is slow and 
hard to scale across borders. Blockchain-based registries offer 
a different approach: once a diploma commitment is saved on-chain, 
anyone can check it independently, without trusting a single database.

The main obstacle is cost. On Ethereum, every permanent storage 
write costs about 20{,}000~gas. If each diploma is stored one by one, 
a university issuing hundreds or thousands of diplomas per graduation 
cycle faces very high fees. The question is therefore not \emph{whether 
blockchain} can be used for credentials, but \emph{how diplomas 
should be stored on-chain so that costs stay reasonable}.

This paper addresses that question using \emph{UniVerify}, a prototype 
diploma-verification system consisting of a Solidity smart contract and 
an off-chain TypeScript SDK. Universities act as issuers who publish 
diploma commitments on-chain, while verifiers check a diploma by providing 
the document hash and any necessary cryptographic proofs. Two fundamental 
approaches are compared: (1)~\textbf{Per-record storage}, where each 
diploma is saved in its own mapping slot and verified in constant time; 
and (2)~\textbf{Merkle batch issuance}, where one root covers an entire 
graduation class, and diplomas are verified via off-chain proofs.

The main contribution of this work is a practical cost evaluation of 
real, deployable smart contracts. Specifically, this paper provides:
\begin{enumerate}
  \item A cost comparison of packed per-record storage and Merkle-batch 
        issuance across deployment, issuance, verification, and revocation.
  \item A four-way benchmark that separates architectural, library, 
        and EVM-level effects.
  \item A closed-form linear gas-savings model validated at six proof 
        depths with errors below 0.2\%.
  \item An economic analysis translating gas savings into real dollar 
        costs for institutions, including market sensitivity analysis.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Related Work}
\label{sec:related}

Grech and Camilleri~\cite{grech2017blockchain} identified
credential verification as one of the main uses of
blockchain in education.
Lizcano et~al.~\cite{lizcano2020decentralised} proposed
a decentralized credential system using IPFS and Ethereum
but did not analyze gas costs in detail.
Alketbi et~al.~\cite{alketbi2018blockchain} surveyed
blockchain applications in government services, including
education records.
The W3C Verifiable Credentials
standard~\cite{w3cvc2024} defines a data model for
tamper-proof claims but does not address cost.
Arndt et~al.~\cite{arndt2023onchain} measured on-chain
credential storage on Ethereum~L2 networks but did not
compare Merkle batching with per-record storage.

Merkle trees~\cite{merkle1987} are the standard way to
prove that an element belongs to a set.
OpenZeppelin's \texttt{MerkleProof}~\cite{openzeppelin}
is the most widely used Solidity library for this purpose.
Other approaches---sparse Merkle trees, Verkle
trees~\cite{verkle}, RSA accumulators---are not yet
practical for typical EVM contracts.

Ethereum execution costs are defined by the EVM gas
schedule~\cite{yellowpaper}, and EIP-2929 increased
costs for first-time storage access~\cite{eip2929}.
Most earlier work on blockchain credentials focuses on
architecture, privacy, or governance.
This paper takes a different approach: it measures how
specific Solidity design choices affect the gas cost
of issuing and verifying diplomas in an implemented contract.

\section{System Design and Methodology}
\label{sec:method}

\subsection{Architectures and Contract Variants}

As illustrated in Fig.~\ref{fig:arch}, two fundamental architectures 
were evaluated. In \textbf{Architecture A (Per-Record Storage)}, each 
diploma is stored in a \texttt{mapping(bytes32\,$\Rightarrow$\,uint256)}. 
The issuer address (160~bits) and a revocation flag (1~bit) are packed 
into one 256-bit slot. Issuing a diploma requires one \texttt{SSTORE}, 
and verifying it requires one \texttt{SLOAD} plus bit masking, 
yielding a constant read cost. In \textbf{Architecture B (Merkle Batch)}, 
all diplomas in a graduation class form a Merkle tree off-chain. Only the 
32-byte root is written on-chain, covering the whole batch in a single 
storage write. Verification requires the caller to provide 
$d{=}\lceil \log_2 N \rceil$ sibling hashes. Each leaf utilizes domain 
separation to prevent cross-chain replay attacks.

To isolate the architectural effects from implementation choices, 
four Solidity contract variants were built (Table~\ref{tab:variants}). 
\Vpacked{} implements the per-record storage baseline. \Vbase{} uses a 
hand-written Merkle proof loop with \texttt{abi.encodePacked}. \Voz{} 
utilizes the widely adopted OpenZeppelin \texttt{MerkleProof} library. 
Finally, \Vopt{} introduces targeted EVM-level optimizations on the 
verification path to test the limits of gas reduction.

\subsection{Optimization Techniques and Experimental Setup}

The \Vopt{} variant incorporates four low-level techniques to 
minimize verification costs. \textbf{T1 (Unchecked loop):} 
\texttt{unchecked\{++i;\}} removes arithmetic overflow checks during 
proof iteration. \textbf{T2 (Scratch-space hashing):} Inputs are placed 
directly in EVM scratch memory and hashed in place, avoiding memory 
allocation overhead. \textbf{T3 (Validation splitting):} Public functions 
validate inputs, while internal paths skip redundant checks. 
\textbf{T4 (Assembly preimage):} The 112-byte leaf preimage is packed 
using \texttt{mstore}/\texttt{shl} instead of Solidity's native encoding.

All measurements were performed using Foundry~\cite{foundry2023} with 
Solidity~0.8.20 and the optimizer set to 200~runs. Proof depths 
$d \in \{0,1,4,8,10,12\}$ were evaluated, corresponding to batch sizes 
from 1 to 4{,}096 diplomas. Function-level gas consumption was 
extracted directly from Forge test reports.

\subsection{Gas Consumption Analysis}

At $N{=}100$, Merkle issuance is 98.9\% cheaper per diploma.

\subsection{Economic Impact and Analytical Model}

To contextualize these gas metrics, we apply an economic projection 
assuming a gas price of $g{=}40$~gwei and $p{=}\$2{,}500$/ETH. 
This yields an issuance cost of \$4.82 per diploma for \Vpacked{} 
and \$5.09 for one total Merkle batch root. At an institutional scale, 
publishing 1{,}000 diplomas costs \$4{,}820 with per-record storage, 
compared to just \$5.09 with batching. 

To fully capture this architectural advantage against cryptocurrency volatility, 
Table~\ref{tab:econ} presents a sensitivity matrix of batch issuance costs under 
varying network states. Even under high network congestion (80~gwei, \$4{,}000/ETH), 
the 1{,}000-diploma batch cost remains under \$17, solidifying the model's robustness.

The gas savings achieved by \Vopt{} over \Vbase{} can be mathematically 
decomposed into a linear model. Let $d$ be the proof depth:
\begin{equation}
\Delta(d) =
  \underbrace{d \cdot 199}_{\text{T1+T2: per proof step}}
  +\underbrace{144}_{\text{T3+T4: constant}}
\label{eq:model}
\end{equation}
The linear coefficient (199~gas) corresponds precisely to the per-iteration 
savings from bypassing the \texttt{ADD} opcode overflow check (via \texttt{unchecked}) 
and avoiding memory expansion costs by hashing in the pre-allocated EVM scratch space. 
The constant term (144~gas) reflects the fixed savings from the unified assembly 
preimage setup (\texttt{mstore}/\texttt{shl}) and reduced jump destinations via 
validation splitting. Table~\ref{tab:model} and Fig.~\ref{fig:model} confirm that 
this model matches measured gas values with exceptional accuracy, showing a residual 
error below 0.2\% across all tested depths.

\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:discussion}

The core engineering trade-off in credential registries lies between 
issuance and verification efficiency. The \Vpacked{} baseline offers 
the cheapest verification, but its issuance cost grows linearly with 
the number of diplomas. Merkle batching reverses this dynamic: 
issuance remains virtually constant at the batch level, but each 
verification requires an off-chain proof. For academic institutions, 
this trade-off is highly favorable. Issuance is a paid, on-chain 
operation borne by the university, while verification is generally 
treated as a read-only operation executed via local nodes or RPC 
endpoints, costing zero real ether.

Based on the benchmark results, a clear hierarchy of optimization 
impact emerges. At the highest level, the architectural switch to 
Merkle batching dominates, cutting per-diploma issuance costs by 
up to 98.9\%. At the mid-level, library selection plays a role, with 
OpenZeppelin's \texttt{MerkleProof} providing a 3.1\% reduction in 
verification gas over an unoptimized baseline. Finally, extreme 
low-level EVM tuning (\Vopt{}) yields an additional 6.6\% reduction 
in verification gas and a 10.8\% reduction in deployment costs. 
However, these low-level gains cannot compensate for an inefficient 
storage model, reinforcing that the elimination of per-record state 
writes is the single most critical optimization.

From a practical standpoint, OpenZeppelin's standard library is the 
recommended default for production deployments. It captures the bulk 
of available gas savings while maintaining code readability and ease 
of auditing. Hand-tuned assembly is only justified under extreme 
throughput requirements where verification costs directly translate 
to execution constraints. Conversely, for highly localized, small-scale 
registries updating a handful of records sporadically, a simple packed 
per-record storage approach remains a viable and robust option.

This study possesses certain limitations. Measurements were strictly 
confined to Ethereum Layer 1 gas dynamics and isolated to warm EVM 
storage access (100~gas per \texttt{SLOAD}) using Foundry. EIP-2929 
cold-access surcharges on mainnet impose an initial 2{,}100~gas penalty 
per slot read. While this would increase absolute verification costs 
for all variants, it does not alter their relative ranking, as both 
architectures require at least one cold storage read to fetch either 
the credential root or the per-record flag. Future research should expand 
this evaluation to Layer 2 networks, calldata-aware designs under 
EIP-4844, and forthcoming Verkle-tree state architectures.

% =============================================================
\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}

This paper evaluated two deployable architectures for on-chain diploma 
verification and presented a closed-form model predicting optimization 
savings across variable proof depths. The empirical evidence demonstrates 
that architectural design supersedes code-level optimization in reducing 
Ethereum execution costs. For universities issuing credentials in cohorts, 
Merkle batching decreases the per-diploma issuance cost by up to three 
orders of magnitude compared to traditional per-record storage mappings. 
As academic registries increasingly migrate to blockchain infrastructure, 
these findings establish Merkle-based issuance combined with standard 
cryptographic libraries as the most economically viable framework, ensuring 
that decentralized verification remains sustainable at scale.
